Methodology

What EdgePulse does, how, and what it cannot do.

Data

Daily closing prices of front-month futures from Yahoo Finance, one source and one unit per market. Nothing is mixed in from other providers, because switching sources mid-series creates fake jumps.

Wheat and corn trade in cents per bushel and are shown in dollars. A price is dropped only when it jumps sharply for a single day and immediately reverses, which is almost always a data error. Front-month futures roll to the next contract periodically, which can add small artificial moves.

The {{ coverage_pct }}% one-month range

EdgePulse does not publish a price target. For commodities, a simple "price stays the same" guess is very hard to beat, and the previous version of this site proved it by losing to exactly that.

Instead it answers a question that matters for budgets and contracts: how far could this price realistically move in the next {{ horizon_days }} days?

  1. Current volatility. An exponentially weighted average of recent daily price changes (decay 0.94), so recent turbulence counts more than calm months a year ago.
  2. The market's own history. For every past day, the {{ horizon_days }}-day move that followed is divided by that day's volatility. Only moves whose outcome was already known on the forecast date are used.
  3. The range. The 5th and 95th percentiles of those standardized moves, scaled by today's volatility, give the low and high prices. Fat tails and skew in each market are captured from its own data instead of assuming a bell curve.

If the method is well calibrated, about {{ coverage_pct }}% of outcomes land inside the range. The track record shows how close it gets.

The trend signal

"Uptrend" means today's price is above where it was {{ trend_days }} trading days ago (about three months). It is shown without a strength rating, because the backtest gives no reason to trust one. It describes where the price has been. As a predictor of the next month it has not beaten always guessing "up" in the backtest, and the track record shows that openly.

The track record therefore compares it with two baselines: a coin flip (50%) and always predicting "up". A trend signal that cannot beat "always up" adds nothing.

Scoring

  • Live forecasts are made every weekday from that day's close and stored immediately, starting {{ live_start }}.
  • Backtests run the identical code on past data, one forecast a week per market, using only prices available on each date.
  • A forecast is scored against the first closing price on or after its due date. The scoring happens in the database. Live forecasts are write-once: a database rule rejects any attempt to change or delete one after it is stored, and the daily job only adds new prices without rewriting old ones.
  • Forecasts overlap, because each one looks {{ horizon_days }} days ahead and a new one is made every week or weekday. The scored counts therefore overstate how many independent tests there are.
  • The backtest is not a blind test. The model was built and adjusted while that history was available, so only live forecasts are a real out-of-sample test.

Limitations

  • Volatility estimates react with a lag. Ranges are often too narrow right after a sudden shock, such as a war or supply disruption.
  • The trend signal is a weak indicator, not a prediction. Do not base a trade or a contract on it alone.
  • Futures prices are not the prices a business pays. Freight, grade, basis and currency differences can be large.
  • Yahoo Finance data is free and unofficial. Gaps and occasional errors are possible.
